<p>I told you in my first and second posts on this topic that the traditional rule in Georgia was the &#8220;first bite rule&#8221; and that rule was often viewed as a &#8220;one free bite&#8221; rule. I have also discussed how that rule has changed over recent decades.</p>
<p>One rationale behind the traditional &#8220;one free bite&#8221; rule was that the owner of a dog should not be blamed for an unforeseen and unforeseeable act of an animal. Proof of prior knowledge of the dog&#8217;s &#8220;vicious propensity&#8221; should be required before holding an owner responsible for injuries caused by the animal.</p>
<p>Recent court decisions, while upholding the rationale behind the traditional Georgia rule, have moved toward softening the application of it. Recent decisions have tended to replace the traditional rule with a new rule that places a duty on the owner to take &#8220;reasonable steps&#8221; to protect the public from a dog if the owner has &#8220;superior knowledge&#8221; of the dog&#8217;s vicious tendency even if the dog has never bitten in the past.</p>
<p>These recent court decisions have focused less on requiring the injury victim to prove a prior similar incident or attack and more on the owner&#8217;s knowledge of the dog&#8217;s aggressive temperament. Thus in recent years we have seen a relaxing of the traditional requirement that the injured person prove a prior similar incident.</p>
<p>Another avenue injured persons have used to hold an owner liable for injuries caused by a dog is a legal theory called &#8220;negligent undertaking.&#8221; The reasoning behind this theory is that if an owner voluntarily agrees to restrain a dog and then fails to do so properly and an injury results, the owner can be held liable even if there is no evidence of &#8220;vicious propensity or a violation of a leash law or ordinance.</p>
<p>Dog bite cases are very fact specific &#8211; by which I mean the outcome depends a great deal on many facts that are discovered after date of the bite itself as the case moves forward. The cases that interpret Georgia&#8217;s dog liability statute can be difficult to apply to a given set of facts.</p>
