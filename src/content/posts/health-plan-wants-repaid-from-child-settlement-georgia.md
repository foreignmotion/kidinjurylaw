The letter usually comes from a company you have never heard of. It is not your insurance company. It is a recovery vendor hired by your employer’s health plan. It says the plan paid your child’s medical bills after the injury, that the plan has a “lien” on any recovery, and that you must not settle without paying it back.

Sometimes the number on that letter is bigger than the insurance money available to your child.

A few weeks ago I wrote about [whether to use your health insurance or your MedPay](/2026/11/16/health-insurance-or-medpay-child-medical-bills-georgia-wreck/) after a wreck. That post explains Georgia’s complete-compensation rule and why self-funded employer plans can get around it. This post picks up where that one stopped: the bills are paid, the case is settling, and the plan wants its money. What now?

## Step one: find out what kind of plan it really is

Everything depends on this, so I repeat it here.

**If the plan is fully insured, or is an individual or Marketplace policy,** Georgia’s statute applies. Under O.C.G.A. § 33-24-56.1, the plan may be reimbursed only if your child has been fully and completely compensated for all losses, and even then its recovery is reduced by its share of the attorney’s fees and expenses. In a case where the at-fault driver had a small policy and your child was badly hurt, that often means the plan gets nothing.

**If the plan is a self-funded employer plan governed by ERISA,** federal law generally lets the plan’s own written terms control instead. *FMC Corp. v. Holliday*, 498 U.S. 52 (1990); *US Airways, Inc. v. McCutchen*, 569 U.S. 88 (2013).

**Some employer plans are not ERISA plans at all.** ERISA does not cover government plans or most church plans. If you are a teacher, a state employee, or a county or city employee, your plan may be a governmental plan. The State Health Benefit Plan, which covers most Georgia teachers and state employees, is self-insured but is not an ERISA plan. Whether Georgia’s statute reaches it is a question your lawyer needs to answer before anyone pays it.

Recovery vendors do not always get this right. Some letters claim ERISA protection for plans that are fully insured. Make them prove it.

Medicaid and PeachCare follow their own rules, which I covered in [a separate post](/2026/10/30/medicaid-peachcare-liens-georgia-child-injury-settlement/).

## Step two: get the plan document, not the brochure

Even a true ERISA plan can only collect what its written terms allow. So the first thing I ask for is the plan itself.

Under ERISA, you have the right to request the plan’s governing documents in writing from the plan administrator. The administrator must provide them within 30 days, and a court can impose a penalty of up to $110 a day for failing to do so. 29 U.S.C. §§ 1024(b)(4), 1132(c)(1).

Send the request to the plan administrator named in the Summary Plan Description. That is usually your employer, not the insurance company whose name is on your card.

What I look for:

- **Does the plan document itself contain the reimbursement language?** Sometimes it appears only in the summary handed to employees. That can matter.
- **Does it reject the “common fund” rule?** In *McCutchen*, the Supreme Court held that when a plan is silent about attorney’s fees, the plan must share in the cost of the lawyer who created the recovery. Clear plan language can override that. Silent or vague language cannot.
- **Does it reject the “made whole” rule?** Most modern plans do, expressly. Older or sloppier ones sometimes do not.
- **Who does it bind?** Look for language about dependents and minors.

## Step three: check the bills

The plan’s itemized list of payments is often wrong. I regularly find:

- well-child visits, vaccines, and sick visits that have nothing to do with the injury;
- treatment for a different injury or illness;
- charges listed twice; and
- the billed amount instead of what the plan actually paid.

The plan is entitled, at most, to what it actually paid for treatment of this injury. Every unrelated line comes off.

## Step four: whose money is it?

When a child is hurt in Georgia, [there are two claims](/2026/10/23/your-childs-claim-and-your-claim-who-owns-what-georgia-child-injury/). The parent’s claim covers the medical bills while the child is a minor. The child’s claim covers pain and suffering and everything else that belongs to the child.

Your health plan covers you as the employee, and your child as your dependent. Most plans now say their reimbursement rights reach any recovery by a dependent, including a minor. Whether a plan can enforce that against money that belongs to a child who never agreed to anything is a question courts have answered differently.

At a minimum, how the settlement divides money between your claim and your child’s claim matters. That division has to be honest and supported by the facts. A judge approving a child’s settlement will look at it.

## Step five: the plan has to be able to reach the money

A self-funded plan enforces reimbursement through a lawsuit in equity under ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3). In *Sereboff v. Mid Atlantic Medical Services*, 547 U.S. 356 (2006), the Supreme Court allowed a plan to enforce an equitable lien on settlement money that was still identifiable. In *Montanile v. Board of Trustees*, 577 U.S. 136 (2016), the Court held that a plan could not recover from a participant’s general assets after the settlement money had been spent on things that could not be traced.

Do not read *Montanile* as a strategy. When a plan has given notice of its claim, a lawyer who is holding the money cannot simply pay it out and hope. Under Georgia’s ethics rules, a lawyer has to protect funds that a third party has a legitimate claim to until the dispute is resolved. Plans have also sued the lawyers who paid out the money.

What *Montanile* does show is that the plan’s rights are not unlimited, and that the plan has its own reasons to negotiate.

## Step six: negotiate, and get it in writing

Very few of these claims are paid at the full number on the first letter. A plan’s willingness to reduce its claim goes up with:

- a small insurance policy that does not come close to covering your child’s losses;
- the cost of the lawyer who made the recovery possible;
- the child’s future medical needs, which the plan may not cover once your job changes;
- Georgia’s 2025 tort reform. For injuries after its effective date, a jury can be told what the health plan actually paid, not just what the hospital billed. The plan’s discount can lower the value of your child’s case. I explained the change in [my post on SB 68](/2026/10/28/georgia-2025-tort-reform-sb-68-what-it-means-when-your-child-is-hurt/).

Any reduction must be in writing before any money is paid out.

## Where the judge comes in

In Georgia, a settlement of a minor’s claim above a set amount needs court approval, and a large net recovery may require a conservatorship. The judge will want to know who is being paid out of the settlement and why. I have written about [how court involvement works](/2022/07/15/legal-strategies-to-minimize-or-avoid-court-involvement-in-georgia-child-injury-settlements-part-i/), [how to keep it to a minimum](/2026/10/26/legal-strategies-to-minimize-or-avoid-court-involvement-in-georgia-child-injury-settlements-part-ii/), and why [a minor settlement is not binding until a court approves it](/2017/11/28/inadequate-minor-settlement-not-enforceable-until-judicial-approval/).

A reimbursement claim that would take most of a child’s recovery is exactly the kind of thing a judge should hear about.

## What I tell parents

- Do not ignore the letter. Plans can stop paying for your child’s ongoing care, and they can sue.
- Answer the plan’s accident questionnaire truthfully, but do not sign a separate reimbursement agreement or “lien acknowledgment” until a lawyer reads it.
- Request the plan document from the plan administrator, in writing.
- Keep every Explanation of Benefits.
- Send every letter from the plan or its recovery vendor to your lawyer.
- Do not settle your child’s case without knowing, in writing, what the plan will accept.

The plan paid real money for your child’s care, and sometimes it is entitled to some of that money back. But it is entitled only to what the law and its own written terms allow. Make it show you both.
