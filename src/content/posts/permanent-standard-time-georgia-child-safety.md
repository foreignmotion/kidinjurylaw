Earlier this month I wrote about [a new study on permanent daylight saving time and dark school mornings](/2026/11/11/dark-school-mornings-bus-stop-child-pedestrian-georgia/). Of the 14 states the researchers calculated, Georgia’s increase in dark school trips was the largest.

That post asked who answers when a child is hit at the bus stop. This one asks a different question. If darker mornings put children at risk, why not go the other way? Why not stay on standard time, normal time, all year long?

I expected the answer to be that it was a nice idea that no one could actually do. That is not what I found. Georgia could do it on its own, without asking Congress. And I think it should, for the sake of our kids.

## The choice no one in Georgia is talking about

Most of the fight over the clocks is about permanent daylight saving time. Georgia passed a law in 2021 to adopt it, but only if Congress allows it. The U.S. House passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July. It has stalled in the Senate. Earlier this year, Georgia’s Senate also passed a bill to move the state to the Atlantic time zone, which would have put us on daylight saving time all year by another route. It died in the House.

Here is the part that gets lost. Federal law already lets a state stay on standard time all year. Under 15 U.S.C. § 260a, a state that lies entirely within one time zone may by law exempt itself from daylight saving time, as long as the whole state observes standard time. Arizona, outside the Navajo Nation, does it. So does Hawaii.

Georgia lies entirely within the Eastern time zone. The General Assembly could pass that law in January. No one in Washington has to approve it.

It is not even a new idea here. In 2021, the Georgia Senate voted 46-7 for Senate Bill 100, which called for Georgia to observe standard time year round. The House rewrote the bill to call for permanent daylight saving time instead, and that is the version that became our law, waiting on Congress ever since.

## What standard time all year would mean for a Georgia school morning

On standard time, the sun comes up in Atlanta at about 7:43 on the darkest January mornings. Under permanent daylight saving time, it would be about 8:43.

Standard time does not make every winter morning bright. Many Georgia children will still wait in the dark on the earliest buses. But it means one less hour of darkness every winter school morning than permanent daylight saving time would bring. Over a school year, in a state of Georgia’s size, that is the difference the University of Washington researchers measured: tens of millions of school trips in the dark, or not.

The same researchers found that a fatal crash involving a school-aged pedestrian was roughly twice as likely on dark mornings as after sunrise. They also said that permanent daylight saving time would undo the benefits of later high school start times, which were adopted because teenagers’ body clocks run later.

## We tried permanent daylight saving time once

In January 1974, during the energy crisis, the country went on daylight saving time all year. It was popular when it started.

Then children started going to school in the dark. The New York Times reported that eight Florida schoolchildren were killed in accidents in the weeks after the change. Florida’s governor asked Congress to undo the law. Congress repealed it that October.

Be fair about what that proves. A Department of Transportation study later found that morning deaths of school-age children were higher than the year before, but could not blame the increase on daylight saving time alone. Even so, the country tried it, parents saw their children walking to school in the dark, and the country quit within the year.

## The spring change itself is dangerous

There is a second reason to pick standard time, and it has nothing to do with which clock is better. Every spring, we take an hour of sleep from every driver in America on the same night.

Researchers at the University of Colorado looked at more than 700,000 fatal crashes. They found that fatal crashes rose about 6 percent in the workweek after the spring change, about 28 more deaths a year. The effect was strongest in the morning hours. Standard time all year ends that change for good.

## The honest objection: darker evenings

I said I would argue for this, and I will. But I will not hide the best argument on the other side.

Evening light matters too. More people are on the road in the evening rush than at dawn, and some studies have found that moving daylight into the evening reduces pedestrian deaths. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety found that pedestrian and bicyclist deaths go down when we switch to daylight saving time in the spring, while deaths of people inside vehicles go up. Its president told the Senate that the evidence does not point definitively one way or the other.

So here is why I still come down on the side of standard time for children.

**The school morning is not optional.** A child has to be at the bus stop at the time the school district sets. Parents cannot move it. Evenings are different. A family can often decide when practice ends, when the walk home happens, and whether someone drives.

**School still lets out in daylight.** On standard time, the sun sets in Atlanta at about 5:33 in late December. Most children are home from school well before that.

**The people who study sleep agree.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine calls permanent standard time the best choice for health and safety. In 2023, its position was endorsed by 20 medical, scientific, and advocacy organizations, including the National PTA and the National Safety Council.

And whatever we do with the clocks, the answer to dark evenings is the same as the answer to dark mornings. The IIHS president put it plainly: dark roadways are deadly no matter the time. Light the crosswalks. Slow the cars down near schools and neighborhoods. Watch for children.

## Is this pie in the sky?

Maybe. No one I know of is pushing a permanent standard time bill in Georgia right now. Permanent daylight saving time is popular, and “more sunshine” is an easy thing to vote for. “Darker summer evenings” is not.

But this is the rare child safety problem that Georgia can fix by itself, with one law, at no cost. It does not need a new program, a new agency, or a dime of tax money. It only needs the legislature to choose the clock that gets children to school in the light more often.

I spent my own childhood walking to the school bus in the dark, half a mile at one house and a mile down a dirt hill at another. I know what those mornings felt like. I would rather Georgia give its children more light in the morning, not less.

If you agree, tell your state representative and your state senator. Ask them to keep Georgia on standard time all year, and to do it for the kids.
