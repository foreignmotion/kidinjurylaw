Thanksgiving and Christmas put a lot of children in houses that were not built for them. There is the pool nobody fenced because the grandchildren live three states away. The uncle’s dog that “never bites.” The stairs with no gate, the gun in the nightstand, the space heater in the den, and a dozen cousins running through all of it.

When a child gets badly hurt at a relative’s house, parents usually call me with two questions at once. Who is going to pay for this? And do I really have to sue my own mother to find out?

This post answers both.

## The short answer: you are claiming against the insurance, not against Grandma

Most homeowner’s policies, and most renter’s policies, include liability coverage. That coverage exists for exactly this: a guest is hurt on the property, and the guest claims the owner was careless.

When you make a claim, the insurance company investigates it, hires and pays a lawyer to defend your relative if a lawsuit is ever filed, and pays any settlement or verdict up to the policy limits. In most of these cases, your relative does not pay a dime.

There is one hard truth I tell families up front. If a lawsuit has to be filed, Georgia law requires it to be filed in your relative’s name, not the insurance company’s. The jury will not be told about the insurance. That is uncomfortable, and you should talk it through with your family before it happens. But most of these claims settle without a lawsuit, and many families find that once Grandma understands her insurance company is the one paying, the awkwardness passes.

## There may also be a small no-fault benefit

Many homeowner’s policies include a separate “medical payments to others” coverage. It pays a guest’s reasonable medical bills regardless of fault, usually up to a few thousand dollars.

It is not much, but it can pay an ER copay or an urgent care bill without anybody having to prove anything. Ask about it early. It usually does not cover people who live in the household.

## What Georgia law says the homeowner owed your child

This is where holiday visits get legally tricky.

**A social guest is a “licensee.”** Georgia treats people who come to your home for a social visit as licensees, not invitees. That matters, because the owner’s duty to a licensee is narrower. Under O.C.G.A. § 51-3-2, an owner is generally liable to a licensee only for willful or wanton injury. In practice, Georgia courts have read that to require the owner to warn of hidden dangers the owner knows about, and to use ordinary care not to hurt a guest once the owner knows the guest is there.

Your child’s age does not change that label. Georgia courts have said a person’s status as a trespasser, licensee or invitee does not depend on age.

**But the analysis does not stop there.** Three other rules often matter more than the licensee label.

**1. Did the relative take charge of your child?** If you went to the store and left your daughter with her aunt, the aunt was not just a homeowner. She was the person supervising your child. Someone who agrees to watch a child takes on a duty to use reasonable care in watching that child. Courts look at whether the adult actually assumed that job. In one case, the court found no evidence that the homeowners had undertaken to supervise the child, which is the question that decides many of these cases.

**2. Attractive nuisance.** In *Gregory v. Johnson*, 249 Ga. 151 (1982), the Supreme Court of Georgia adopted the attractive nuisance rule of the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 339. A landowner can be liable to a young child for an artificial condition, like a pool, when the owner knows children are likely to be around it, the danger is one young children will not appreciate, and the burden of making it safe is small compared to the risk. A pool, by itself, is not automatically an attractive nuisance, but an unfenced pool in a house full of visiting toddlers is a strong case. I have written more about [drowning claims](/2020/07/31/child-death-or-injury-drowning/) and [Georgia pool rules](/2016/05/03/georgia-pool-rules-child-injury-in-swimming-pools-spas-and-recreational-water-parks/).

**3. Dogs.** Georgia’s dog bite statute, O.C.G.A. § 51-2-7, has its own rules, and they do not depend on whether your child was a guest. I explained them in [my dog bite post](/2020/09/08/child-attacked-by-dog-in-georgia-what-are-your-legal-options/). The family dog is the single most common reason I get these calls.

## What about grandparents? Isn’t there a family immunity?

Georgia has parental immunity. An unemancipated child generally cannot sue a parent for simple negligence. Insurance companies sometimes try to stretch that to grandparents.

It usually does not stretch that far. Georgia courts have said that grandparents, simply as grandparents, do not stand in the place of parents. In *Harris v. Hardman*, the court refused immunity to a grandmother whose grandchild was only visiting overnight.

It is different when the grandparent is really raising the child. Courts have applied immunity to a grandfather who took in the child and her mother (*Maddox v. Queen*, 1979), to a grandmother who was the child’s legal guardian (*Clabough v. Rachwal*, 1985), and to grandparents a child had been placed with (*Bennett v. Bennett*, 1990).

So the question is not “Is she the grandmother?” It is “Was she acting as the parent?” A Christmas visit almost never makes a grandparent a parent.

## When the child who caused it was a cousin

Sometimes the injury comes from another child: a shove off the trampoline, a BB gun, a golf cart driven by a 12-year-old cousin.

Children can be responsible for their own conduct, depending on age. Parents are not automatically responsible for their children’s carelessness, but there are exceptions. Georgia makes parents liable for a child’s willful or malicious acts, up to a limit set by statute, and parents can be liable for their own negligence, such as handing a child a gun or the keys. I covered that in [When Can a Parent Be Held Liable for the Conduct of Their Child?](/2014/01/01/when-can-a-parent-be-held-liable-for-the-conduct-of-their-child/) and in [my post on e-bikes and golf carts](/2026/11/04/e-bikes-golf-carts-kids-georgia-who-pays/).

The cousin’s parents’ homeowner’s policy may cover it. So may the policy at the house where it happened. Often both.

## The traps

**Your own household.** If your child lives in the house where the injury happened, as many children living with grandparents do, the homeowner’s policy almost certainly excludes injuries to members of the household. Read that section with a lawyer before assuming there is nothing.

**Late notice.** Homeowner’s policies require prompt notice. Families often wait months because nobody wants to “make it a thing,” and then the insurer argues the delay cost it the chance to investigate. Your relative should report the injury to the insurance agent soon, even if no one has decided to make a claim.

**The cooperation clause.** Your relative has to cooperate with the insurance company and tell it the truth. Families sometimes worry that Grandma will “take the blame” to help the child, or that she will be pressured to blame you. Neither is acceptable. Everyone should tell the truth, and only the truth. A claim built on a story that changes is a claim that fails.

**“Where were you?”** Expect the insurer to ask whether you, the parent, were supposed to be watching. I wrote a whole post about [that question](/2026/11/02/can-the-insurance-company-blame-you-georgia-child-injury/).

**Recorded statements.** You do not have to give one to your relative’s insurance company. Read [what three words in a recorded statement can cost](/2026/11/09/recorded-statement-insurance-company-georgia-child-injury/) before you agree to one.

## Keeping the family together

I have represented a lot of children against the insurance of people who love them. The ones that go best share a few things.

- Somebody talks to the relative early and explains that the claim is against the insurance policy, not against her.
- The relative reports the injury to the insurance agent right away.
- Nobody discusses fault on the family group text.
- The settlement stays within the policy limits, so the relative’s own savings are never at risk.

That last one matters. When the insurance company has a fair chance to pay the claim within its limits and refuses, Georgia law can make it responsible for a verdict above the limits. A good lawyer for your child protects your relative from that risk by giving the insurer a fair, documented chance to settle.

## What I tell parents

- Get your child treated, and keep every bill and record.
- Take photos of the condition: the pool gate, the stairs, the dog’s yard, the heater.
- Get the name of the homeowner’s insurance company and the policy number from your relative. Ask about medical payments coverage.
- Ask whether anyone else at the house has insurance that might apply, including the parents of any child involved.
- Do not give a recorded statement before you get advice.
- Remember your own deadline. Your child’s claim and your claim for the medical bills [are not on the same clock](/2026/10/12/georgia-minor-tolling-statute-your-childs-deadline-is-not-your-deadline/).

Making a claim is not the same as accusing your mother of anything. It is using the insurance she has been paying for, for the exact thing she bought it for.
