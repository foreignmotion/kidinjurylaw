School lets out, and the roads fill up with teenagers. They have time, friends home from college, holiday parties, and late nights. For a 16-year-old with a new license, it is often the most driving with friends in the car they have ever done.

When one of those trips ends in a wreck, the first question parents ask me is simple: whose insurance pays? The answer is almost never just one policy. It depends on who was driving, whose car it was, and who caused the wreck.

## First, the rules your teenager is driving under

Georgia gives 16- and 17-year-olds a Class D provisional license under the Teenage and Adult Driver Responsibility Act. Under O.C.G.A. § 40-5-24, a Class D driver:

- cannot drive between midnight and 5 a.m., with no exceptions;
- during the first six months, cannot carry any passenger who is not immediate family;
- during the second six months, cannot carry more than one passenger under 21 who is not immediate family; and
- after that, cannot carry more than three passengers under 21 who are not immediate family.



Those rules exist because the risk to a new driver goes up with every teenage passenger. They also matter after a wreck. Breaking a safety statute can be evidence of negligence, but it does not decide a case alone. The violation still has to have something to do with why the wreck happened. Breaking the passenger rule also does not, by itself, cancel the insurance.

## If your teen was a passenger in a friend’s car

This is the most common call I get over the holidays. Your son rode with a friend, and the friend wrecked. Several layers of insurance may be available.

**The car’s liability coverage.** In Georgia, insurance generally follows the car. The policy on the car the friend was driving is usually first in line, as long as the friend had permission to drive it.

**The driver’s own coverage.** If the friend was driving a car that was not on his family’s policy, his family’s policy may provide additional coverage on top of the car’s policy.

**The friend’s parents.** Under Georgia’s family purpose doctrine, a parent who provides a car for the family’s use can be responsible for a family member’s negligent driving of it. I explained the doctrine in [my post on e-bikes and golf carts](/2026/11/04/e-bikes-golf-carts-kids-georgia-who-pays/). It adds a defendant, not usually more insurance, because the same policy typically covers the parents.

**Your own uninsured motorist coverage.** This is the one families forget. Your teenager is probably an insured under your own auto policy’s uninsured motorist (UM) coverage, even riding in someone else’s car. Since 2009, Georgia UM coverage is ordinarily “added on.” It pays on top of the at-fault driver’s liability coverage, not reduced by it, unless you chose the reduced type in writing. O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11. When the friend’s policy is a $25,000 minimum policy and your child’s injuries are serious, your own UM coverage is often where the real money is.

Be careful how you settle with the friend’s insurance company. In Georgia, the wrong release can wipe out your UM claim. I wrote about exactly that in [Barker v. Muschett](/2026/10/15/barker-v-muschett-georgia-limited-release-um-uim-claims/).

## If your teen was driving and someone else caused it

The analysis is similar. The at-fault driver’s liability coverage pays first, and your own UM coverage is there for the amount it does not cover. Your own MedPay can help with medical bills while you wait. I explained how to choose between [health insurance and MedPay](/2026/11/16/health-insurance-or-medpay-child-medical-bills-georgia-wreck/).

## If your teen was driving and caused the wreck

This is the hardest call, and families often do not want to make it. But insurance exists for this too.

**Friends in the car.** A friend hurt in your car will look to your policy’s liability coverage. If you provided the car, the family purpose doctrine may name you as well. That is what your liability coverage is for. Report the wreck to your insurer right away.

**Brothers and sisters in the car.** Many auto policies contain a household or “family member” exclusion, which limits or removes liability coverage for injuries to people who live in the insured’s household. The Supreme Court of Georgia has held that these exclusions are not automatically void. Courts have generally enforced them above the minimum limits Georgia requires, which suggests the minimum coverage is still available.

Do not assume there is no coverage because the injured child is family. Get the actual policy, not just the declarations page, and have a lawyer read it.

## Children who are home from college

An 18-year-old freshman home for Christmas is a legal adult. The claim belongs to the student, not to you, and the ordinary two-year deadline applies.

The bigger question is whether your college student still counts as a “resident relative” under your auto policy. Many policies cover unmarried children who are temporarily away at school. Others are less clear, and Georgia courts have treated residency as a fact question that depends on the policy wording, the student’s intent to come home, and whether the student has set up a separate home.

Before your student goes back in January, read the definition of “family member” or “resident relative” in your policy. If it is unclear, ask your agent to put the answer in writing.

## Parties and alcohol

In Georgia, the general rule is that drinking, not furnishing alcohol, causes alcohol-related harm. But O.C.G.A. § 51-1-40(b) makes an exception. A person who knowingly furnishes alcohol to someone under 21, knowing that person will soon be driving, can be liable for the harm the drunk driver causes. Simply owning the house where teenagers drank is not enough.

If a teenager was drinking at someone’s house before the wreck, tell your lawyer right away. The host’s homeowner’s policy may be another source of coverage. Georgia also gives a parent a separate claim against someone who furnishes alcohol to the parent’s minor child under O.C.G.A. § 51-1-18.

## Phones

Georgia’s hands-free law, O.C.G.A. § 40-6-241, bars drivers from holding a phone while driving. After a wreck involving a teen driver, phone records are often the most important evidence in the case, and they do not last forever. A lawyer can send a preservation letter right away.

## Seat belts

After Georgia’s 2025 tort reform, a jury can now hear whether an injured person was wearing a seat belt. Teenage passengers are some of the least likely people in a car to buckle up. I explained the change in [my post on SB 68](/2026/10/28/georgia-2025-tort-reform-sb-68-what-it-means-when-your-child-is-hurt/). Tell your teenagers before break that it now matters in court as well as in the car.

## What I tell parents before break

- Look at your declarations page tonight. Know your liability limits, your UM limits, and whether your UM is added on or reduced.
- Make sure every licensed teenager in the house is listed on the policy.
- Consider an umbrella policy if you have a teen driver.
- Talk with your teenager about the passenger limits and the curfew. They are the law, not your rules.
- Make the seat belt rule absolute.

## What I tell parents after a wreck

- Get medical care first.
- [Get the other driver’s insurance at the scene](/2026/11/13/get-other-drivers-insurance-at-the-scene-georgia-wreck-child-injury/). Do not count on the police report.
- Report the wreck to your own insurer, even if your teenager was a passenger. You may need your UM coverage.
- Do not let your teenager give a recorded statement to anyone’s insurance company. Read [what three words can cost](/2026/11/09/recorded-statement-insurance-company-georgia-child-injury/).
- Keep your teenager off social media about the wreck. Here is [what not to post](/2026/11/18/social-media-after-child-injury-georgia-what-not-to-post/).
- Do not sign anything from an insurance company before a lawyer reads it, especially a release.
- For a minor, remember that [your claim for the medical bills is not on your child’s clock](/2026/10/12/georgia-minor-tolling-statute-your-childs-deadline-is-not-your-deadline/).

I have lost count of the families who told me they never knew they had UM coverage until after the wreck. Find out now, while your teenager is still asleep upstairs.
