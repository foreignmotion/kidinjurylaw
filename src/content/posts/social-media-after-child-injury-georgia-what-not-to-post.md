When a child is hurt, parents reach for their phones. They want to tell family. They want prayers. They want help with meals and rides. Teenagers post because that is what teenagers do.

The insurance company is reading too.

Adjusters and defense lawyers look at social media in injury cases. They are not looking to see how your child is doing. They are looking for anything they can use to pay less.

## What they look for

**A smile.** Your daughter broke her leg in the wreck. Three weeks later, you post a picture of her blowing out birthday candles, grinning. You were glad to see her happy for one afternoon. The insurance company sees a child who is “doing fine.” It will not see the night she cried from the pain, because you did not post that.

**Activity.** A picture of your son at the beach, at a ballgame, on a trampoline, or back on the field. Even a picture of him standing can be held up to argue his injury is not what you say it is.

**Words about the wreck.** “We’re all okay, thank God.” “I never even saw him coming.” “I only looked away for a second.” Anything you write about how it happened, or how badly your child was hurt, can be used the same way [a recorded statement](/2026/11/09/recorded-statement-insurance-company-georgia-child-injury/) is used.

**Anything that blames you.** Where you were, what you were doing, who was watching your child. Insurance companies [look for ways to blame the parent](/2026/11/02/can-the-insurance-company-blame-you-georgia-child-injury/).

None of this has to be true to cause trouble. A single picture, taken out of context, can take a long time to explain.

## “My account is private” does not protect you

Privacy settings keep strangers out. They do not keep a lawsuit out. If your child’s case is filed, the other side can ask for your posts, your messages, and your photos through the discovery process, and a court can order them produced if they bear on the case. A friend can screenshot a post and pass it along. A relative can share it.

Treat everything you post as if the insurance adjuster will read it, because one day the adjuster may.

## Do not delete what is already there

Your first instinct may be to go back and delete everything. Do not do that without talking to your lawyer first.

Once a lawsuit is reasonably foreseeable, Georgia law can put a duty on a party to preserve evidence. *Phillips v. Harmon*, 297 Ga. 386 (2015). Destroying evidence after that point is called spoliation, and a court can punish it. A jury may be told to assume that what you deleted would have hurt your case. A deleted photo of a smiling child can do far more damage than the photo itself ever would have.

There is a difference between deleting a post and making your account more private, or simply going quiet. Ask your lawyer which is right for you.

## Your teenager is the biggest risk

A teenager who was hurt will want to post about it. So will their friends. Teenagers post constantly, on more apps than most parents know about, and they post things they would never say to an adult.

Sit down with your teenager and explain why this matters. Ask them to stop posting about the wreck, the injury, and what they are doing while they recover. Ask them not to send messages about it either. Group chats and direct messages are not as private as they think. Ask their friends to leave your child out of their posts for a while.

If your teenager was driving, this matters even more. Teenagers are often [unfairly blamed](/2019/09/02/teenage-car-injury-death-legal-claims/) for a wreck. Do not let a joking post give the insurance company the excuse it wants.

## Fundraising pages and family updates

Many families set up a fundraising page, a meal train, or a page to keep friends and family updated on a child’s recovery. That help can mean the world to a family. You do not have to give it up.

Just be careful what you write there. Stick to what the family needs and how people can help. Leave out how the wreck happened and who was at fault. If you post medical updates, be accurate, and do not make your child’s condition sound better or worse than it is. What you write should match what the doctors are writing in the medical records, because the insurance company will compare them.

## The other side’s social media can help your child

It works both ways. The driver who hit your child may post about the wreck too. Look at the other driver’s public pages, and save screenshots with the date showing. Do not contact the other driver, comment on their posts, or send friend requests. Just save what is public and give it to your lawyer.

The same goes for news stories and community pages about the wreck. Witnesses sometimes comment there. Save those too, and do not comment yourself.

## What I tell parents to do

- Stop posting about the wreck, the injury, and your child’s activities until the case is over.
- Do not delete anything without asking your lawyer first.
- Tighten your privacy settings, and do not accept friend requests from people you do not know.
- Talk to your teenager today. Then talk to grandparents, aunts, and uncles. They post too.
- Keep fundraising and family updates to what you need. Leave out how it happened.
- Save screenshots of anything the other driver, or a witness, posts about the wreck.
- Keep your notes about your child’s recovery off social media. Write them down privately and share them with your lawyer.

Your friends and family want to help. The best help they can give right now is a phone call, a meal, and a ride to physical therapy, not a post.
