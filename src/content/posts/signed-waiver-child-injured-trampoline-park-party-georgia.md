You have done it a hundred times. The trampoline park. The climbing gym. The birthday party venue. The summer camp, the rec league, the field trip form. Somebody hands you a tablet or emails you a link, and before your child can go in, you scroll to the bottom and tap “I agree.”

Then your child gets hurt. And the first thing the business says is: “You signed a waiver.”

Many parents stop right there. They assume that tap ended any claim their child had. It may not have.

## What the waiver says

Most waivers say some version of the same thing. You understand the activity is dangerous. You give up any claim against the business if your child is hurt, even if the business was careless. Many go further. They make you promise to pay the business back if your child ever sues it. Many send any dispute to private arbitration instead of a courtroom.

That is what the business wants the waiver to do. What it can actually do under Georgia law is another question.

## Georgia law on waivers in general

Between adults, Georgia courts will often enforce a waiver of claims for ordinary carelessness. But Georgia courts read these waivers strictly against the business. To be enforced, the waiver’s language must be explicit, prominent, clear, and unambiguous. Fine print that hides the point, or language that does not clearly say it releases the business’s own negligence, can fail.

And there are limits no waiver can get past. A waiver generally cannot release a business from liability for gross negligence, or for willful or wanton misconduct. A business that ignored known dangers, or broke basic safety rules, may not be able to hide behind the form.

## Can a parent sign away a child’s claim before the child is hurt?

This is the real question, and it is not settled in Georgia. Georgia’s appellate courts have not squarely decided whether a parent’s signature on a waiver before an injury bars the child’s own claim. Courts in other states have split. Some say a parent can make that choice for a child. Others say a parent cannot give away a child’s rights before anyone knows what the injury will be.

There are strong reasons a Georgia court should protect the child.

**Georgia does not let parents settle a child’s claim freely even after the injury.** When a settlement of a child’s claim is more than $25,000, a parent cannot make it binding on the child without a court’s approval. O.C.G.A. § 29-3-3. [A settlement that should have been approved and was not is not enforceable](/2017/11/28/inadequate-minor-settlement-not-enforceable-until-judicial-approval/). It would be strange if a parent could not settle a broken leg for a fair price without a judge, but could give away every possible claim, for nothing, with one tap on a screen at the front desk.

**Georgia protects children from their own contracts.** A minor’s contract is generally voidable. O.C.G.A. § 13-3-20.

**The child is the one who owns the claim.** The pain, the scars, the lasting injury belong to your child, not to you. [The money belongs to the child](/2026/10/23/your-childs-claim-and-your-claim-who-owns-what-georgia-child-injury/), too.

None of this guarantees how a court will rule in your child’s case. It does mean that “you signed a waiver” is the beginning of the conversation, not the end of it.

## What your signature may cost you

The waiver may be weaker against your child. It may be stronger against you.

You have your own claim when your child is hurt: the [medical bills you paid or owe](/2026/10/23/your-childs-claim-and-your-claim-who-owns-what-georgia-child-injury/) until your child turns 18. You signed the waiver for yourself as an adult. The business will argue it bars your claim even if your child’s survives.

Read the waiver for two other things:

- **An indemnity clause.** That is a promise to pay the business back if your child sues it. A business may use it to try to make the child’s claim come out of the parent’s pocket.
- **An arbitration clause.** That sends the dispute to a private arbitrator instead of a judge and jury. Georgia courts often enforce these.

## When your teenager signs

Many places let teenagers sign their own waivers, or check a box that says a parent approved. Teenagers sometimes sign a parent’s name.

That can go badly. In *Smith v. Adventure Air Sports Kennesaw, LLC*, 357 Ga. App. 1 (2020), a 17-year-old signed his father’s name to a trampoline park’s agreement without his parents’ knowledge, and was badly hurt. The Court of Appeals held he could not use his age to get out of the agreement he had signed in his father’s name, and the case was sent to arbitration.

Talk to your teenager about this before it comes up. Tell them never to sign your name to anything.

## The waiver does not cover everyone

A waiver usually protects the business that wrote it. It may not protect anyone else. If your child was hurt because a piece of equipment was defective, the company that made it did not get a waiver from you. If a different company maintained the equipment or ran the event, it may not be covered either. Do not let the waiver stop you from asking who else was responsible.

## What I tell parents to do

**Before your child goes:**

- Read the waiver. At least read the parts in bold and capital letters.
- Keep a copy. Screenshot it, or save the confirmation email.
- Ask the business about its safety rules and supervision. Look at the place yourself.

**If your child is hurt:**

- Get your child care first.
- Report the injury to the business and ask for a copy of the incident report. Ask for a copy of what you signed.
- Take pictures of the place where it happened, and the equipment, before anything changes.
- Get the names and phone numbers of witnesses and staff.
- Do not give a [recorded statement](/2026/11/09/recorded-statement-insurance-company-georgia-child-injury/) to the business’s insurance company.
- Do not assume the waiver ended your child’s claim. Talk to a lawyer.

The business wrote the waiver to protect itself. It was not written with your child in mind. Before you accept what it says, find out what it can actually do.
