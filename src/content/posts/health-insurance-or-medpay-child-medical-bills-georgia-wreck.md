The bills start coming within days. The ambulance. The emergency room. The radiologist who read the scan. The follow-up with the pediatrician, the orthopedist, the physical therapist.

The other driver’s insurance company is not going to pay any of them. Not now. In Georgia, the at-fault driver’s insurer pays once, at the end, when the claim settles. That can be many months away.

So who pays in the meantime? For most families, the answer is your own coverage. And for many families, the question of *which* of your own coverages to use is harder than it looks.

## Where the money comes from while you wait

Georgia is not a no-fault state. There is no required personal injury protection coverage that pays your bills regardless of fault. Instead, the money in the meantime usually comes from one or more of these:

- **Your health insurance**, or your child’s Medicaid or PeachCare.
- **Medical payments coverage (“MedPay”)** on your own auto policy, if you bought it. MedPay pays medical bills for people hurt in your car, and often for your family members hurt in someone else’s car or on foot, regardless of who was at fault. The limits are usually small, a few thousand dollars, sometimes more.
- **MedPay on the car your child was riding in,** if it was someone else’s car.
- **Your own pocket,** or a provider who agrees to wait for payment until the case settles.

The bills for your child’s care are your claim, not your child’s. Georgia law makes parents responsible for a child’s medical care, so [the parent holds the claim to recover those expenses](/2026/10/23/your-childs-claim-and-your-claim-who-owns-what-georgia-child-injury/). How you pay them now affects how much of that claim your family keeps later.

## The trap: “reimbursement rights”

Here is what many parents do not know. Whoever pays your child’s medical bills now may expect to be paid back out of the settlement later.

In plain English, a **reimbursement right** works like this. The plan pays the hospital today. The fine print says that if your family later recovers money from the driver who caused the wreck, the plan gets its money back out of that recovery.

Medicaid and PeachCare have that right, and I have [written about how much they can take](/2026/10/30/medicaid-peachcare-liens-georgia-child-injury-settlement/). Private health plans and MedPay often claim it too. But they do not all play by the same rules.

## Georgia’s rule protects families. Some plans get around it.

Georgia has a strong rule on reimbursement. Under O.C.G.A. § 33-24-56.1, a plan that paid medical expenses may be reimbursed out of a personal injury recovery only if the injured person has been **fully and completely compensated** for all of their losses. If the settlement does not make the family whole, there is no reimbursement. Even when reimbursement is allowed, it is reduced by a share of the attorney’s fees and expenses it took to get the money.

Most injury cases settle for less than everything they are worth, because the at-fault driver’s insurance is limited, or because the case has risks. So in Georgia, that rule often means a family owes the plan little or nothing.

The Supreme Court of Georgia has applied that complete compensation rule to MedPay. *Thurman v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 278 Ga. 162 (2004). For a MedPay carrier to get paid back, it has to clear that hurdle. In my experience, that is a hard burden for a MedPay carrier to meet.

Now the catch. Many large employers do not buy insurance for their employees. They pay employees’ medical claims out of their own money and hire an insurance company only to process the paperwork. These are called **self-funded ERISA plans**, after the federal law that governs employee benefits.

Federal law shields self-funded ERISA plans from state insurance rules like Georgia’s. *FMC Corp. v. Holliday*, 498 U.S. 52 (1990). That means the plan can write its own reimbursement rule, and the plan’s own words control. The Supreme Court of the United States has held that if the plan’s terms clearly say so, it can be paid back dollar for dollar, even when the family was not made whole and even before the attorney’s fees are taken out. *US Airways, Inc. v. McCutchen*, 569 U.S. 88 (2013).

Plans for federal employees are similar. Their contracts can override state rules on reimbursement. *Coventry Health Care of Missouri, Inc. v. Nevils*, 581 U.S. 87 (2017).

## So should you use your health insurance?

This is a tricky calculus, and there is no single answer for every family.

**If your health plan is a self-funded ERISA plan, or another plan with strong reimbursement rights, it MAY be better not to run your child’s wreck-related bills through it,** if you have MedPay or some other way to pay them. Every dollar the plan pays may be a dollar it takes back out of the settlement, in full. A dollar MedPay pays is much harder for the MedPay carrier to get back.

But that decision has real costs, and you need to weigh them:

- **MedPay runs out fast.** A few thousand dollars may not cover one night in a hospital. Once it is gone, the bills still need to be paid.
- **Health insurance gets a discount. MedPay usually does not.** A health plan pays the reduced rate it negotiated with the hospital. Without it, the hospital may bill its full price.
- **There are deadlines for billing health insurance.** Most plans require providers to submit claims within a set time. If you wait too long to decide, the option may be gone.
- **Hospitals can file liens.** A Georgia hospital that is not paid can file a lien against your family’s injury claim. O.C.G.A. § 44-14-470.
- **Medicaid and PeachCare work differently.** If your child has Medicaid or PeachCare, different billing and repayment rules apply. Do not try to apply this post to Medicaid without talking to a lawyer.

And one rule that has no exceptions: **never delay your child’s care over a billing question.** Get the treatment. Sort out who pays afterward.

## How to find out what kind of plan you have

You cannot make this decision without knowing what kind of health plan you have. I wish I could tell you that is an easy thing to find out. It is not.

This is a technical question, and even attorneys struggle with it sometimes. Whether a plan is self-funded, and whether its language is enough to give it a reimbursement right that overrides Georgia law, is something plans and families fight over in court again and again. A few words in a long plan document can decide it.

Do not take the plan’s word for it, either. Plans want to be paid back. Sometimes a plan, or the company it hires to collect for it, will claim to be a self-funded ERISA plan when it is not, because that is the way to get around Georgia’s rule. A letter that says “this plan is governed by ERISA” does not make it so.

You can start gathering the pieces yourself:

- **Ask your employer’s HR office** whether the plan is “fully insured” or “self-funded” (sometimes called “self-insured”).
- **Ask for the Summary Plan Description** and the full plan document. Look for a section called “Reimbursement,” “Subrogation,” or “Third-Party Liability.”
- **Look at the insurance card.** If the insurance company is described as the “claims administrator” and not the insurer, that is a sign the plan may be self-funded.
- **Find your auto policy’s declarations page** and look for “Medical Payments.” Check the limit. Check the policy of any car your child was riding in.

But the bottom line is this: **get a lawyer to investigate what kind of plan you have.** Give your lawyer those documents, and let your lawyer decide how the bills should be paid.

## What I tell parents to do

- Get your child the care your child needs. That comes first, always.
- Report the wreck to your own auto insurer, and ask whether you have MedPay and how much.
- Have a lawyer find out whether your health plan is really self-funded before you decide how to route the bills.
- Keep every bill, every explanation of benefits, and every letter that mentions “reimbursement,” “subrogation,” or “lien.”
- Do not sign a reimbursement agreement a plan sends you without having a lawyer read it.
- Talk to a lawyer early. The way the bills are paid in the first weeks can decide how much your family keeps at the end.

The other driver’s insurance company caused this problem by making you wait. Do not let the way you pay the bills while you wait cost your family a second time.
